**Short answer:** an econometrics course grades three kinds of work: derivations and problem sets, regression code in Stata, R or Python, and written judgment about what an estimate means and whether it can be read as causal. AI tools can help with all three, so a general AI policy leaves too much open. Your policy should say which of these students must do themselves, how they show the reasoning behind a result, and how you want them to use AI when they are allowed to. Pick one of the three policies below, then add the econometrics clauses that fit your course.

## What makes econometrics different

- **Code produces a table, not an argument.** An AI tool can write regression code that runs and prints a clean table. A student who cannot read the code cannot tell whether the sample, the controls or the standard errors are what the question asked for.
- **Interpretation and identification are the core skills.** Saying what a coefficient means, why it may be biased and what assumption would make it causal is where students show understanding. It is also where fluent AI text is easiest to paste in and hardest to grade.
- **Empirical projects run over weeks.** Many courses end with a project on real data, from cleaning to write-up. Your policy should cover each stage, and it should say whether the data may go into an AI tool.

## Three sample policies

Copy the one closest to your course and edit the bracketed parts.

### 1. No generative AI on graded work

> Generative AI tools (for example ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot) may not be used to write code, solve problems, choose specifications or write interpretations for any graded problem set, project or exam in this course. The goal of [course] is for you to learn to set up an empirical question, estimate it and judge what the estimates mean, and these tools skip that step. You may use course materials, software documentation and help from the teaching staff. If you are unsure whether something is allowed, ask before you submit.

### 2. AI for studying, not for submitted work

> You may use generative AI tools to study, for example to have a concept such as omitted variable bias explained step by step, to generate practice questions, or to understand an error message in code you wrote. Use them as a tutor: ask for explanations and hints after you have tried the problem, not for answers. You may not use them to produce code, derivations or written answers that you submit. Everything you hand in should be work you can explain line by line. If you used an AI tool while studying for an assignment, add one sentence at the end saying how.

### 3. AI allowed with disclosure

> You may use generative AI tools on [problem sets / the empirical project], including to draft or debug code, as long as you disclose it. For each assignment, list the tool, what you used it for and the key prompts. You are responsible for every line of code and every claim in your submission: check that the code estimates the model you intend and that the output supports your interpretation. Interpretations and discussion of identification must be written in your own words. AI use is not allowed on [exams / in-class assessments].

## Clauses worth adding for econometrics

- **Explain-your-code check.** "I may ask you to walk me through any part of your submitted code or output. If you cannot explain it, it will be treated as work that is not your own."
- **Interpretation in your own words.** Even under a permissive policy, require that the meaning of the main estimate and the threat to identification be written by the student. A short paragraph is enough and quick to grade.
- **Say how to use AI for learning.** If you allow AI for studying, give examples of tutor-style use, such as asking for an explanation after an attempt rather than the answer. The study below suggests this kind of guidance matters.
- **What to disclose and what data may go in.** Name the tool, what it was used for and the prompts that shaped the answer, or attach the chat log ([KU CTE](https://cte.ku.edu/building-ai-policies-your-syllabus)). If students work with restricted or research data, say that it may not be pasted into an AI tool.

## What one recent experiment found

Sebastian Gallegos ran two randomized interventions in a required third-year undergraduate econometrics course at Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez in Chile, across seven coordinated sections with about 300 students ([IZA Discussion Paper 18513, 2026](https://docs.iza.org/dp18513.pdf)). Students had access to a course-specific AI study assistant built from earlier semesters' lecture notes, problem sets, exams and solutions.

- Encouraging students to adopt the assistant before the midterm raised awareness and reported use but did not improve midterm performance.
- Guidance on learning-oriented use before the final exam shifted students toward tutor-style use, raised final-exam performance by 0.21 standard deviations and raised the chance of a passing exam grade by 12 percentage points.

This is a working paper, not yet peer reviewed, from one university and one course, and it tested a course-specific assistant rather than a general chatbot. The author notes the findings are specific to that setting. The practical takeaway for a policy: if you allow AI for studying, telling students how to use it may matter more than permission alone.
